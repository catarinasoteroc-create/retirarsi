Medicinal Plants for Digestion: An Herbalist's Guide

The plants that have been supporting human digestive systems for centuries — and how to bring them into your kitchen, your cup, and your daily rhythm.

By Kate  ·  October 2026  ·  6 min read

Good digestion is not glamorous. It doesn't make for compelling social media content. But after years of working with people in a wellness context — in retreats, in workshops, in 1:1 sessions — I've come to believe that it is one of the most foundational things you can tend to. Not because the digestive system is the whole of health. But because when it isn't working well, almost nothing else works well either.

The body's ability to transform food into energy, to absorb nutrients and eliminate what isn't needed, is the basis of everything from mood to immunity to energy levels. When that process is disrupted — by stress, by processed food, by rushing through meals without chewing, by a liver and gallbladder that are quietly struggling — the effects ripple outward in ways we don't always trace back to their source.

Plant medicine has been working with digestion for as long as humans have cooked and gathered. The kitchen spice rack of traditional cultures was also the medicine cabinet. What follows is a guide to the plants I return to most — for everyday digestive support and for the specific complaints that come up most often.

"Eating slowly, with the television off, chewing well — this is the underestimated foundation. The plants come after."

The foundation: how you eat, not just what

Before we get to the plants, one thing: the digestive system is deeply sensitive to the state of the nervous system. When we're stressed, rushing, distracted, eating at our desks — the body is not in the mode that allows optimal digestion. The parasympathetic nervous system — rest and digest — needs to be active for digestion to work well. A calm meal, eaten slowly and with attention, without screens, chewing each mouthful properly — this is not a small thing. It is, arguably, the most important digestive intervention available.

From there, choosing organic food where possible, limiting processed foods, eating seasonally — these create the conditions in which the digestive system can do its job. The plants help. But they work best on good soil.

Spices and herbs that support digestion daily

Many of the plants most supportive of digestion are already in your kitchen. Adding them intentionally to meals — not as decoration but as medicine — is one of the simplest things you can do.

Ginger

Zingiber officinale

Warming, stimulating, anti-nausea. Freshly grated into salads, soups or hot water. One of the most researched plants for digestive complaints.

Fennel

Foeniculum vulgare

Excellent for bloating and flatulence. Use the seeds in cooking, add fresh fronds to salads, or steep as a tea after meals.

Peppermint

Mentha × piperita

Relaxes the smooth muscle of the digestive tract, relieves spasm and nausea. A few fresh leaves during or after a meal, or as tea.

Chamomile

Matricaria chamomilla

Gentle and deeply soothing. Good for heartburn, bloating, nervous digestive complaints. One of the first plants to reach for.

Rosemary

Rosmarinus officinalis

Stimulates digestive secretions and liver function. Use in cooking, especially with heavier dishes. Abundant in Arrábida, where it grows wild.

Sage

Salvia officinalis

Traditional digestive tonic, particularly useful after fatty meals. A few leaves in cooking or infused as tea.

Turmeric

Curcuma longa

Anti-inflammatory, liver-protective, supports bile production. Add to food regularly — absorption improves with black pepper and healthy fat.

Cardamom

Elettaria cardamomum

An Ayurvedic digestive spice par excellence. Warming, carminative, pleasant. Add to coffee, rice dishes, warm drinks.

A simple habit: add savory (Satureja hortensis) to legumes while cooking. This traditional practice significantly reduces the flatulence that often accompanies beans and lentils — one of those pieces of kitchen herbalism wisdom that deserves wider knowledge.

Teas for before and after meals

A cup of herbal tea before or after a meal can do a great deal. It slows the rhythm of eating, it prepares the digestive system, and it delivers plant medicine in one of the most bioavailable forms. The teas I recommend most for everyday digestive support:

Digestive teas worth keeping at home

  • Chamomile — for bloating, heartburn, digestive cramps, nervous stomach
  • Fennel seed — for flatulence and bloating, especially after meals
  • Peppermint — for nausea, indigestion, spasm; also headache related to digestion
  • Lemon verbena — gentle and aromatic, calming for the digestive nervous system
  • Fresh ginger — for nausea, motion sickness, cold digestion (simmer slices in water 10 min)
  • Lemon balm / Melissa — for nausea related to emotional stress or anxiety

The Arrábida landscape is full of medicinal plants — rosemary, lavender, wild fennel, thyme. Learning to read them is part of what we do in retreat.

For nausea, heartburn and bloating

These three complaints come up most often. A few specific notes:

Nausea — Ginger is well-supported by research for nausea of almost any cause: morning sickness, motion sickness, post-operative nausea. Peppermint works well too, particularly when nausea comes with headache. A drop of peppermint essential oil on a cloth or cotton ball, inhaled slowly, is quick and effective. For nausea with an emotional component — anxiety, grief, overwhelm — lemon balm tea is the gentler choice.

Heartburn — Chamomile and mallow are the traditional plants here. Both are anti-inflammatory and soothing to the mucosa. If heartburn is persistent or severe, it warrants professional assessment — but for occasional mild discomfort after a heavy or acidic meal, chamomile tea is a reasonable first response.

Bloating and flatulence — Fennel, chamomile, ginger and peppermint all help. The Japanese kukicha tea (three-year tea, made from roasted twigs of the tea plant) is a traditional remedy for improving stomach and intestinal function that many people find surprisingly effective.

The liver and gallbladder

Sometimes poor digestion traces back to the liver and gallbladder not producing sufficient bile. When that's the case, fats in particular become difficult to digest, and a meal that should be fine leaves you heavy and uncomfortable. Two plants are particularly well-regarded for liver and gallbladder support:

Artichoke leaf (Cynara scolymus) — one of the most researched plants for liver protection and digestive support. Stimulates bile production, protects liver cells, relieves indigestion. Available as tea, tincture or supplement.

Dandelion root (Taraxacum officinale) — another classic liver and gallbladder tonic. The root in particular stimulates bile production and supports the liver's detoxification processes. The fresh leaves are also excellent in salads — bitter, mineral-rich, and used in traditional food cultures precisely because bitter foods stimulate digestive secretions.

"The bitter taste is one of the most digestively supportive — and one of the most absent from modern diets."

A word about the plants growing around us

One of the things I love about working in the Arrábida is how many of these plants grow wild around us. Rosemary, wild fennel, lavender, chamomile — you can smell them as you walk. There's something about learning to see them in their natural context — understanding their ecology, the season they flower, the part of the plant that's used — that changes your relationship to plant medicine.

It stops being something you buy in a health food shop and becomes something you notice, gather, dry, prepare. That shift in relationship is, I think, part of what makes herbalism different from simply taking supplements. It reconnects you to something.

We explore this on our retreats in Arrábida — walking the landscape with attention, learning what's growing and why, bringing some of what we find back to prepare together. If that interests you, the Inner Compass retreat in April includes herbalism as one of its three pillars.
